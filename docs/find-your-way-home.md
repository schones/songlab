# Find Your Way Home

## An untimed, game-based introduction to major-key ii–V–I progressions

Learner: A curious pianist who knows note names and basic intervals but mixes up seventh-chord qualities and their roles.

Goal: Build, recognize, and connect a major-key ii–V–I, then transfer the pattern to another key.

Format: Five lessons, repeated as needed. Answers can come from an on-screen keyboard or a real instrument. All activities are untimed: no countdowns, time targets, or speed-based advancement.

Core game loop: Hear it → copy it → complete it → recall it → transfer it.

# 1\. Change the chord, keep the root

Learning goal: Distinguish major seven, dominant seven, and minor seven by the notes that change.

Start on C. Cmaj7 contains C–E–G–B: a major third and major seventh. C7 contains C–E–G–B♭: a major third and minor seventh. Cm7 contains C–E♭–G–B♭: a minor third and minor seventh.

## Game rounds

1. Show and play Cmaj7; the learner copies it.  
2. Ask: “Change this to C7 by moving one note.”  
3. Ask: “Now change C7 to Cm7 by moving one note.”  
4. Reverse the changes, then mix the prompts.

Keep the root and fifth visibly anchored. Highlight the changed note after an answer.

Feedback example: “You lowered B to B♭. That makes C7. To make Cm7, lower E to E♭ too.”

Ready to advance: The learner makes the changes in both directions without a note-name hint and explains which note changed.

# 2\. Give each chord a job

Learning goal: Separate a chord’s root, quality, and role in a key. Keep “Home: C major” visible throughout.

ii: Dm7 begins the phrase. V: G7 points toward home. I: Cmaj7 arrives home. Teach the phrase: “Two minor, five dominant, one major.”

## Game rounds

1. Give all three roots—D, G, C—and ask the learner to assign their qualities.  
2. Play and display the complete phrase; the learner copies it.  
3. Hide one chord and ask the learner to complete the phrase.  
4. Show only “Home: C major” and ask for the whole progression.

Initially accept the correct chord tones in any order; teach particular voicings separately.

Feedback example: “G is the right root. Here it has the dominant role, so its seventh is F, not F♯.”

Ready to advance: The learner completes the phrase without hints and identifies ii, V, and I.

# 3\. Find the smallest movement

Learning goal: Experience voice leading through thirds and sevenths. Supply or play the roots in the bass while the learner plays two-note shapes.

Dm7: lower F (third), upper C (seventh). G7: lower F (seventh), upper B (third). Cmaj7: lower E (third), upper B (seventh).

## Game rounds

1. Copy each two-note shape.  
2. Hold F and move C down to B.  
3. Hold B and move F down to E.  
4. Connect all three shapes without visual guidance.

Show two note paths so the learner can see which voice stays and which moves.

Teaching point: In these major-key ii–V–I transitions, the seventh falls a half step to the next chord’s third. The other note stays and changes its role. This is not a rule that every seventh in every cycle progression must move a half step.

Feedback example: “Keep F where it is. Only C needs to move—to B.”

Ready to advance: The learner connects the shapes and points out the seventh-to-third movement at both transitions.

# 4\. Move the destination

Learning goal: Transfer the pattern while keeping the chord roles intact. Introduce only one new key.

Home C: Dm7 → G7 → Cmaj7. Home F: Gm7 → C7 → Fmaj7.

## Game rounds

1. Announce “Home is F.”  
2. Give the roots G–C–F; ask for their qualities.  
3. Copy the complete phrase.  
4. Complete missing chords, then recall the phrase.  
5. Alternate between C and F.

Use C as a comparison: Cmaj7 is I in C; C7 is V in F. The letter name alone does not determine the quality.

Feedback example: “For a complete ii–V–I in F, start on Gm7. Cm7 begins a ii–V–I in B♭.”

Ready to advance: The learner builds both phrases and explains why the C chord changes quality.

# 5\. Travel around the cycle

Learning goal: Practice complete phrases in successive keys. Reveal one destination at a time.

C → F → B♭ → E♭ → A♭ → D♭ → G♭ → B → E → A → D → G → C

The move from G♭ to B uses B as the familiar enharmonic spelling of C♭.

## For each destination

1. Name home.  
2. Find the ii and V roots.  
3. Assign minor seven → dominant seven → major seven.  
4. Play the phrase.  
5. Connect the thirds and sevenths.

Let the learner pause between keys for as long as needed. Connecting phrases with a steady pulse may be an optional musical exercise, but it is never a time target or advancement requirement.

Teaching point: The key centers travel around the cycle. Every adjacent chord root does not need to follow the cycle across phrase boundaries.

# Feedback and progression rules

Chord-quality difficulty: Keep the root fixed; change only the third or seventh.

Root-finding difficulty: Keep the destination visible; locate scale degrees 2, 5, and 1\.

Chord-function difficulty: Keep roots visible; assign minor, dominant, and major.

Voice-leading difficulty: Return to two notes; show the stationary note.

Key-change difficulty: Alternate two familiar destinations before adding another.

Reward independent recall and successful correction. Do not score speed or impose a timer. Fade hints gradually and revisit material after a different challenge to check retention.

A successful finish is more than playing the right notes: the learner can say where home is, what job each chord does, and which note moves next.
