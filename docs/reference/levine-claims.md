# Levine reference claims (photographed 2026-10-28)

Source claims for SongLab labelling, parsing and /learn content. Each line is
paraphrased from the cited page. JTB = *The Jazz Theory Book*, JPB = *The Jazz
Piano Book* (Mark Levine). Cite these instead of re-deriving.

## Chord symbols and degree names (JTB p. ix)
- T1. G7alt and G7(♭9, ♯9, ♯11, ♭13) mean the same thing.
- T2. Major 7th: CΔ (also Cmaj7, CM7). Minor 7th: D-7 (also Dm7, Dmi7).
- T3. "-" after the root means minor (D-7).
- T4. "+" and "♯" both raise an alteration (C7+11 = C7♯11); "-" and "♭" both
  lower one (C7-9 = C7♭9). So "-" means minor straight after the root and
  flat in front of an extension.
- T5. 4 and 11 are the same note: say 4 on major and sus chords (CΔ♯4, Csus4),
  11 on dominant and minor chords (C7♯11, C-11).
- T6. 6 and 13 are the same note: say 6 on major and minor chords (C6, C-6),
  13 on dominant chords (C7♭13).
- T7. Many piano voicings for major 7th chords leave out the major 7th.

## Rootless left-hand voicings (JPB ch. 7, pp. 41–45)
- R1. Left-hand voicings usually leave out the root. The bass plays it, and
  pianists use these voicings in solo playing too (p. 41).
- R2. A position, II–V–I in C (fig 7-2):
  - D-7 = 3–5–7–9 (F A C E)
  - G7 = 7–9–3–13 (F A B E)
  - CΔ = 3–5–6–9 (E G A D), with no major 7th, which is why it can be written C69
- R3. Alternative I chord in A position (McCoy Tyner, fig 7-3): 3–5–7–9.
- R4. B position (fig 7-4):
  - D-7 = 7–9–3–5 (C E F A)
  - G7 = 3–13–7–9 (B E F A)
  - CΔ = 7–R–3–5 (B C E G): the root is in the voicing but not on the bottom
- R5. Alternative I chord in B position (fig 7-5): 7–9–3–5. "Use with care":
  on its own it can sound like G6.
- R6. "A" and "B" name a whole II–V–I by the bottom note. In A the bottom
  notes run 3rd–7th–3rd; in B they run 7th–3rd–7th (p. 43).
- R7. II to V: only the 7th of II moves, down a half step, to become the 3rd
  of V. The other three notes stay (p. 42).
- R8. B-position D-7 is two major 3rds a half step apart (fig 7-6).
- R9. Register: practise A position with the little finger between C3 and
  middle C. When the voicing with the 7th on the bottom falls too low (E-7,
  E♭Δ), it sounds "muddy", so switch to the other position (pp. 42, 44–45).
- R10. Spelling check: there's no E natural in G♭, so an E♮ in a D♭7 voicing
  is a sign of a mistake (p. 42).

## Half-diminished (JTB p. 29; ch. 3 pp. ~61, 68–69)
- H1. Bø = B-7♭5, a minor 7th chord with a flatted 5th. It is VII in C
  major, from the Locrian mode (p. 29).
- H2. The name: a diminished 7th is A C E♭ G♭; Aø has G instead of G♭, so it
  is only "half" diminished (p. 68 fn 62).
- H3. On Locrian, the ♭9 is an "avoid" note over ø (fig 3-59). It's fine as a
  passing note but dissonant when struck or held (p. 69).
- H4. Locrian ♯2 (6th mode of melodic minor) has a natural 9 and no avoid
  note. The natural 9 is "arguably the prettiest note you can play on an Aø
  chord" (p. 69).
- H5. Both modes contain ♭5 and ♭6 (♭13), so ♭13 is a scale tone on ø (p. 68).
- H6. Bebop players mostly used Locrian; since the 1960s the trend has been
  toward Locrian ♯2. Many players use both (p. 69).
- H7. Because it has a minor 3rd and minor 7th, ø functions as a II chord
  (p. 68).

## Sus chords (JTB ch. 3 pp. 43–45)
- S1. Gsus goes with G Mixolydian, the same mode as G7 (p. 43).
- S2. A sus chord is "a V chord in which the 4th doesn't sound like an
  'avoid' note." Over G7 the 4th (C) does sound like an avoid note
  (figs 3-25, 3-26).
- S3. "Sus" = suspended 4th. Traditionally the 4th resolves down a half step
  to the 3rd of a dominant 7th; in contemporary music it often doesn't, which
  gives sus chords a floating quality (fig 3-24).
- S4. Common voicing: the root in the bass with the major triad a whole step
  below it in second inversion (F/G for Gsus, fig 3-28). Adding E gives the
  13th of Gsus (fig 3-29).
- S5. The same chord may be written G7sus4, Gsus4, FΔ/G, F/G or D-7/G.
  D-7/G shows its function: a II–V in one chord (p. 45).
- S6. Sus chords function as V chords and resolve to CΔ as smoothly as G7
  does (p. 44).
- S7. History: an everyday sound in jazz since the 1960s; Ellington and
  Tatum used them in the 1930s–40s. Examples: Bernstein's "Some Other Time"
  (1944), Coltrane's "Naima", Hancock's "Maiden Voyage" (pp. 44–45).

## Diminished as dominant (JTB ch. 14 pp. 336–337)
- D1. The four notes of a diminished 7th chord are the 3rd, 5th, 7th and ♭9
  of a V7♭9. Because diminished harmony repeats every minor 3rd, one dim7
  chord is four disguised V7♭9 chords and can resolve four ways. F♯° =
  D7♭9, B7♭9, A♭7♭9 or F7♭9 (resolving to G, E, D♭, B♭).
- D2. Example: bar 2 F♯° in Gershwin's "Embraceable You" (fig 14-105;
  Donald Brown's reharmonisation, fig 14-106, is a copyrighted arrangement
  and not for use).
